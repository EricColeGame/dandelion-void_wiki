## How Playtest Access Usually Works

Most indie teams run playtests in waves rather than opening the floodgates. Wave one is often a handful of trusted testers. Later waves widen to newsletter subscribers, Discord regulars, and finally a public sign-up capped by server capacity or survey quality. That funnel exists for a reason: a build that crashes under ten thousand simultaneous logins teaches the team nothing about their game and everything about their infrastructure.

Because access details shift from wave to wave, treat any single source as a snapshot. The reliable move is to register interest through official channels and let the developer tell you when a window opens. If you want a neutral place to check whether a store page or sign-up form exists yet, the [Dandelion Void listing on Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/search/?term=Dandelion+Void) is a reasonable starting point, since store pages often host playtest sign-up buttons directly.

## What Happens During a Dandelion Void Playtest Session

A well-run session has a shape. It is not just "install and vibe for three hours." Teams usually want a specific slice of the game exercised under specific conditions, because that is what produces comparable data across testers.

Expect a short briefing, a defined play window, and a feedback form at the end. Expect the build to be unpolished — placeholder art, missing audio, and systems that are technically present but not yet tuned. That roughness is not a red flag; it is the point. A Dandelion Void playtest that feels finished is usually a marketing demo wearing a playtest badge.

| Session Phase | Typical Length | What You Do | What to Write Down |
|---|---|---|---|
| Onboarding | 5–10 min | Read the brief, accept terms | Anything confusing in the instructions |
| Guided play | 30–60 min | Complete specific tasks or reach a goal | Where you got stuck, and why |
| Free play | 20–40 min | Explore beyond the script | Bugs, exploits, and pleasant surprises |
| Feedback form | 10–20 min | Answer structured questions | Concrete moments, not vibes |
| Optional debrief | Varies | Voice chat or forum thread | Questions the form did not ask |

### Things That Are Usually Off-Limits

- Recording or streaming, unless the developer explicitly permits it
- Sharing screenshots of unfinished content
- Discussing unreleased mechanics outside the tester space
- Datamining the build files

None of these restrictions are unusual. Breaking them is the fastest way to get quietly removed from future waves, and word travels between small studios.

## How to Give Feedback That Developers Can Actually Use

Most playtest feedback fails for the same reason: it describes a feeling without a cause. "The combat felt bad" is not actionable. "I died three times to the second encounter because the dodge input registered late on my controller" is actionable — it points at a system, a moment, and a symptom.

Write notes while you play, not after. Memory rewrites itself fast, and the frustration you felt at minute twelve will be a vague shrug by minute sixty. Keep a notepad open, timestamp your observations, and record what you expected to happen versus what actually happened.

| Feedback Type | Weak Version | Strong Version | Why It Lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bug report | "It broke" | "Crashed on loading the second area, twice, after ~40 min" | Reproducible and scoped |
| Balance note | "Too hard" | "Third boss took 14 attempts; damage windows felt under 1 second" | Gives a measurable target |
| Feel note | "Controls are clunky" | "Camera lags behind turning; I overshoot corners" | Names the specific system |
| Praise | "Fun!" | "The sound cue for a successful dodge made me want to keep trying" | Tells them what to protect |
| Suggestion | "Add a map" | "I got lost twice; a compass or landmark would have fixed it" | Frames the problem, not the solution |

Rank your issues by severity, not by how annoyed you were. A crash that wipes progress outranks a texture that pops. Developers triage constantly, and a clean severity ranking is a gift.

## Playtest vs. Demo vs. Beta vs. Early Access

These terms get used interchangeably in casual conversation, but they mean very different things — and conflating them leads to mismatched expectations. Here is how a Dandelion Void playtest compares to the other ways a game reaches the public before launch.

| Format | Build State | Who Gets In | Money Involved | Your Feedback Shapes It? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Playtest | Early, unstable | Invited or signed-up testers | Usually free | Yes, heavily |
| Demo | Polished vertical slice | Anyone | Free | Somewhat |
| Closed beta | Feature-complete, buggy | Limited invites | Free | Moderately |
| Early access | Playable, evolving | Anyone who buys | Paid | Yes, over time |
| Full release | Finished | Everyone | Paid | Rarely |

The key distinction: a playtest is a research tool, while a demo is a sales tool. Treating a playtest build like a demo — judging it on polish — misses what the team is actually asking you for.

## Etiquette and Common Mistakes

Community reports from testers across indie projects consistently flag the same handful of missteps. Avoiding them makes you the kind of tester a studio remembers when wave two opens.

- **Do not treat the build as a review copy.** Writing a public verdict on unfinished code is unfair to the team and unhelpful to readers.
- **Do not dump a wall of text.** Ten well-described issues beat sixty one-liners.
- **Do not assume the fix.** Describe the problem; let the designers choose the solution.
- **Do report the boring stuff.** Crashes, save corruption, and soft-locks matter more than opinions.
- **Do answer the questions asked.** If the survey asks about pacing, do not spend the whole form on the menu font.

One more habit worth building: note your hardware and settings at the top of every report. A performance complaint from a machine with different specs than the developer's test rig is hard to act on without that context.

### A Simple Feedback Template

| Field | What to Put There |
|---|---|
| Build version | The exact identifier shown on the main menu |
| System | OS, GPU, RAM, input device |
| What I did | The steps leading to the moment |
| What happened | The observed result, plainly stated |
| What I expected | The result you assumed you would get |
| Severity | Crash, blocker, major, minor, cosmetic |

Fill that out for each issue and you will produce better reports than the majority of testers in any given wave.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the Dandelion Void playtest free to join?**
Playtests are almost always free — you are providing unpaid research, and studios generally do not charge for that. If a site ever asks you to pay for playtest access, treat it as a scam and walk away.

**How do I know when a new Dandelion Void playtest wave opens?**
Sign up for the newsletter, join the community server, and wishlist the store page. Announcements tend to hit those three places first, and windows can be short.

**Will my progress carry over to the full release?**
Almost never. Playtest saves are usually wiped between builds because the underlying data structures change. Play for the experience and the feedback, not for a head start.

**Can I stream or make videos of the Dandelion Void playtest?**
Only if the developer says so in writing. Many builds run under a confidentiality agreement, and some permit footage only after a specific date. When in doubt, ask before you hit record.
